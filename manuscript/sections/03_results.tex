\subsection{Accuracy and Representability of the QSE Subspace}
In this section, we use statevector simulations with the UCCSD ansatz to study the spectral accuracy and representability of low-lying QSE roots across all active spaces considered. 
The QSE excitation energies are compared against exact CASCI energies within the same active space. 
Unless otherwise stated, we focus on the singlet ground state, $S_0$, together with the lowest singlet and triplet excited states, $S_1$, $T_1$ and $T_2$.
We chose to characterize QSE performance in this low-energy region because the lowest excited states commonly govern key photophysical and photochemical processes, including fluorescence, phosphorescence, and excited-state reactions \cite{Dreuw2026ExcitedStateReview, Mai2020PhotochemistryMiniReview}.

\subsubsection{Overview of statevector QSE performance}

Across the low-lying states, QSE performance tends to deteriorate as the active space is enlarged. 
For the smallest active spaces, $2e2o$, $2e3o$, and $4e3o$, the recovered spectra are nearly exact. 
Beyond these cases, substantially larger errors are observed across the benchmark. 

The near-exact recovery in the smallest active spaces is consistent with the relative dimensionality of the QSE subspace. 
In these cases, the one-body operator pool generates more basis states than there are physically accessible determinants within the relevant spin sector.
The QSE subspace can therefore span most of the relevant Hilbert space, leaving little room for representability error.
This favorable dimensionality does not persist as the active space grows. The number of one-body QSE operators scales polynomially, approximately as $n^2$, whereas the dimension of the full configuration-interaction (FCI) space grows combinatorially with the number of electrons and orbitals. 
Therefore, the fraction of the full configurational space accessible to the generated QSE subspace decreases with system size, providing a natural explanation for the overall deterioration in accuracy.

However, active-space size alone does not fully account for the observed error trends. 
The deterioration in performance is non-uniform, with a particularly pronounced increase in errors between four and five orbital active spaces. 
Furthermore, active spaces with the same number of orbitals can exhibit markedly different performance even when the relative dimensionality of the QSE and exact spaces is unchanged, as seen most clearly for 4e5o and 6e5o.

Beyond active-space dependent trends, the QSE errors also vary substantially between electronic states. The ground state $S_0$ is generally recovered within chemical accuracy, whereas the lowest lying singlet state $S_1$ exhibits the largest errors. 
The low-lying triplet states $T_1$ and $T_2$ are recovered more accurately than $S_1$, although not consistently within chemical accuracy.

\subsubsection{State Dependence of QSE Representability}
Next, we seek to better characterize the state-dependent errors observed above by examining the electronic structure of the exact CASCI states. 
Each CASCI wavefunction is decomposed according to the excitation rank of its constituent Slater determinants relative to the Hartree–Fock (HF) reference. 
We focus in particular on the double-excitation weight, defined as the fraction of the total CI weight arising from determinants that differ from the HF reference by two orbital excitations. 
Doubles are of particular interest because they are the lowest excitation rank not independently represented by the present one-body QSE operator pool. 

The decomposition reveals clear differences between the low-lying states (\cref{fig:si-casci-double-excitation-weight}). 
Across active spaces from $4e4o$ to $8e8o$, the mean double-excitation weight of $S_1$ ranges from $17.42\%$ to $22.34\%$, compared with only $2.06$--$4.67\%$ for $T_1$ and $1.61$--$4.80\%$ for $T_2$. 
The substantially larger double-excitation contribution in $S_1$ is consistent with the closed-shell character of the molecular benchmark.
Within the double-excitation sector, singlet states can contain both paired and non-pair doubles. 
Triplet states, by contrast, can only obtain double-excitation character from the latter class, since a paired double excitation of a closed-shell HF reference produces another closed-shell singlet determinant. 
The available double-excitation manifold is therefore more restricted for triplet states, consistent with the smaller double-excitation weights observed.

That being said, the larger double-excitation weights observed for $S_1$, together with its increased errors, do not imply that the double-excitation weight alone governs QSE accuracy. 
$S_0$, for example, contains a larger doubles contribution than the low-lying triplet states, with a mean weight of $5.42$--$8.62\%$ over the same active spaces, while remaining substantially more accurately recovered. 
The relevant distinction is that the double-excitation structure of $S_0$ is already largely encoded in the optimized UCCSD reference. 
For excited states, however, difficulties arise when the required balance of single and double-excitation character differs from that encoded in the VQE reference.
This is challenging for the one-body QSE operator pool studied, which provides independent access only to one-body excitation directions. 
The double-excitation character can consequently be modified only indirectly and remains largely constrained by the VQE reference. 
As the double-excitation contribution of an excited state increases, the required excitation balance generally differs more from the ground-state reference. 
Such states are therefore generally more difficult to represent accurately within the QSE subspace.

\subsubsection{Molecular Case Studies and Active-Space Effects}
Beyond the broad state-dependent trends, substantial variation in QSE performance is also observed between molecules and across different active spaces for the same molecule. 
We therefore examine several molecular cases to identify the electronic-structure features associated with these deviations.
Since $S_1$ exhibits the largest representability failures across the benchmark, the following case studies focus primarily on this state. 

For each molecule, we first compare the lowest excited QSE root, $q_1$, with the exact CASCI singlet states using wavefunction overlap. 
This diagnostic reveals a particularly consistent pattern for five molecules: butadiene, cyclopentadiene, furan, norbornadiene, and pyrrole. 
For all active spaces $4e4o$ and larger, $q_1$ overlaps most strongly with $S_2$ rather than $S_1$ (\cref{fig:si-q1-best-root-transition-map}), with a median overlap exceeding $99\%$. 
These five molecules all contain two C=C double bonds, and their dominant singlet excitation is characterized by a $\pi \rightarrow \pi^{*}$ transition.
Examination of their corresponding CASCI wavefunctions shows a clear contrast in excitation character: $S_1$ carries strong double-excitation character, with a mean weight of $54.70$--$55.19\%$, whereas $S_2$ remains predominantly singly excited, with $94$--$96\%$ singles character and only $3$--$5\%$ doubles weight.  
The restricted one-body QSE subspace therefore represents $S_2$ more readily than the lower-energy $S_1$ state. 
As a result, the poorly represented $S_1$ state is effectively omitted from the recovered spectrum, while the more accessible $S_2$ state appears as the lowest excited QSE root. 
This root omission accounts for the particularly poor $S_1$ performance of these five molecules, with a mean error of approximately $90 \mathrm{mHa}$.

The molecule-specific variation is also reflected in the sensitivity of QSE performance to the specific orbitals included in the active space. 
Rather than changing smoothly with active-space size, the error can change abruptly when orbitals important to the character of the target state enter the active space.
Benzene provides a prominent example (\cref{fig:benzene-imidazole-case-studies}). 
Its low-lying singlet excitation has predominantly $\pi \rightarrow \pi^{*}$ character.
In the more restricted $4e4o$ and $6e5o$ spaces, the exact CASCI states have a singles weight greater than $99\%$ and negligible doubles weight, with errors in $S_1$ below $0.001 \mathrm{mHa}$.
Once the $\mathrm{LUMO}+2$ orbital enters the active space, however, there is an increase in the double-excitation weight of the exact $S_1$ state to approximately $9\%$. 
This change coincides with an increase in QSE errors to more than $70 \mathrm{mHa}$.

Imidazole provides a complementary example of the same active-orbital dependence. 
Here, the dominant $S_1$ excitation has $n \rightarrow \pi^{*}$ character, involving the $\mathrm{HOMO}-2$ nonbonding orbital and the $\mathrm{LUMO}$ (\cref{fig:benzene-imidazole-case-studies}).
In $4e4o$ and $4e5o$, where the $\mathrm{HOMO}-2$ is excluded, this dominant single excitation is unavailable and the exact state exhibits stronger multiconfigurational character, with approximately $75\%$ singles and $23\%$ doubles weight. 
Upon inclusion of $\mathrm{HOMO}-2$ in the $6e5o$ space, the $n \rightarrow \pi^{*}$ excitation becomes directly accessible, and the double-excitation contribution decreases to approximately $14.94\%$.
This change coincides with a substantial reduction in the QSE error, from approximately $91 \mathrm{mHa}$ to $20.5 \mathrm{mHa}$.

Together, these examples show that active-space-dependent variation in QSE accuracy can be traced to the inclusion of specific orbitals that alter the multiconfigurational character of the target state. 
This provides a direct explanation for the pronounced changes in representability observed within the same molecule across different active spaces. Our results also point to the fact that the accurate implementation of QSE requires a priori knowledge of relevant orbitals and important excitations. This information may not be available if classical benchmarks are computationally out-of-reach.

\subsubsection{Reference Ansatz Dependence of QSE Performance}
We next examine how the choice of variational reference ansatz affects QSE performance using the $6e6o$ active space. 
Since the QSE subspace is generated from the optimized VQE state, differences in reference quality and excitation flexibility can influence the states accessible to the expansion. 
We therefore compare several UCC-based ansätze with varying levels of expressiveness and circuit complexity.

\label{sec:ansatz-dependence}

We find that QSE performance varies with the choice of reference ansatz, with more expressive ansätze generally producing more accurate QSE spectra. 
The paired k-UpCCGSDSinglet ansatz restricts excitations to singles and paired doubles. 
Consequently, this ansatz exhibits the largest errors in the predicted spectrum. 
While increasing the number of layers from $k=1$ to $k=3$ systematically improves its performance, the ground-state error at $k=3$ remains more than an order of magnitude larger than that of the next best ansatz. 
This indicates that increasing the flexibility of the paired ansatz does not fully compensate for the restrictions of its excitation manifold.
In comparison, UCCSDSinglet and UCCSD achieve similar accuracies across the low-lying states. 
Although UCCSDSinglet imposes additional parameter-tying constraints, both ansatz families span the same excitation manifold and yield comparable errors.
UCCGSD, which provides the most flexible excitation manifold considered here, generally produces the lowest errors, albeit at the cost of considerably greater circuit depth. 
These results illustrate the expected trade-off between reference-state expressiveness and circuit complexity.

Furthermore, the influence of the reference ansatz is considerably more pronounced for the ground state than for the excited states. 
For $S_0$, the difference in median error between the paired and unpaired ansätze exceeds an order of magnitude, whereas the corresponding variation for $S_1$, $T_1$, and $T_2$ is substantially smaller. 
This difference can be understood from the composition of the exact CASCI states relative to the VQE reference. 

At the $6e6o$ active space, the exact $S_0$ state has a mean squared overlap of $95\%$ with the optimized VQE reference across the six ansätze considered (\cref{tab:si-vqe-overlap}). 
The ground-state solution therefore depends much more on the quality of the reference wavefunction, and deficiencies in the reference can only be partially corrected by the remaining QSE directions.
In contrast, the maximum squared overlap between the VQE reference and any of the excited states considered remains below $2\%$ (\cref{tab:si-vqe-overlap}).
These states therefore rely predominantly on the additional operator-generated directions, whose greater flexibility can partially compensate for imperfections in the reference and reduce their sensitivity to the choice of ansatz.

Beyond spectral accuracy, the choice of reference ansatz also affects the spin purity of the recovered states.
Spin contamination is observed among the recovered QSE roots, with several higher-energy states exhibiting substantial deviations from the target $S(S+1)$ value, in some cases exceeding 1. 
These larger deviations are concentrated predominantly at higher energies, whereas the lowest-lying states remain comparatively less affected (\cref{fig:si-severe-spin-roots}).
Across the ansätze, the more flexible UCCGSD ansatz exhibits the largest degree of spin contamination, whereas the paired ansätze remain nearly spin pure (\cref{fig:si-spin-contaminated-roots}). 
Importantly, we note that imposing spin adaptation at the ansatz level does not necessarily guarantee improved spin purity in the implemented circuit. 
In particular, UCCSDSinglet exhibits greater spin contamination than UCCSD at low Trotter order, an effect associated with finite-order Trotter errors (\cref{tab:s2-spin-mean-trotter}).
A more detailed analysis of the spin contamination is provided in \cref{sec:si-spin-purity}.

Taken together with the spectral-error analysis above, these results identify UCCSD as the most cost-effective option among the ansätze considered. 
It maintains relatively low spectral errors, exhibits substantially less spin contamination than UCCGSD, and avoids the greater circuit depth associated with the more expressive generalized ansatz.

Having examined the representability and spectral accuracy of the QSE subspace, we next turn to its numerical stability. 
As discussed in the Introduction, very small eigenvalues of the overlap matrix $\mathbf{S}$ can strongly amplify noise, making the generalized eigenvalue problem highly sensitive to perturbations.
Here, we examine the full eigenvalue spectrum of $\mathbf{S}$ (\cref{fig:eigenvalue-spectrum}) to understand why these near-null eigenvectors arise in the first place. 

We first classify the QSE excitation operators into three groups: occupied number operators, occupied-to-virtual (OV) excitations, and the remaining excitation classes. 
Each operator generates a corresponding QSE basis state, allowing the eigenvectors of $\mathbf{S}$ to be characterized according to the operator classes from which their components originate (\cref{fig:eigenvector-composition}). 

A clear relationship emerges between the eigenvalue magnitude and the composition of the corresponding eigenvectors. 
In both the singlet and triplet subspaces, the spectrum contains a well-supported block of eigenvectors with eigenvalues close to unity. 
Across the active spaces considered, these modes are strongly dominated by OV-generated states. 
In the $6e6o$ active space, for example, OV-generated states account for approximately $95$--$99\%$ of their composition. 
The singlet block contains $n_{\mathrm{occ}}n_{\mathrm{vir}}$ such eigenvectors, while the triplet block contains $3n_{\mathrm{occ}}n_{\mathrm{vir}}$, corresponding exactly to the number of OV operators in their respective operator pools. 
The singlet spectrum additionally contains a distinct occupied-number dominated mode, with more than $99.9\%$ number character and a mean eigenvalue of $11.5$ at the $6e6o$ active space. 
Beyond these well-supported eigenmodes, the eigenvalues decrease rapidly, accompanied by a progressive reduction in the contribution from OV-generated states.


This behavior can be understood from how the different QSE operators act on the underlying VQE reference. 
Because the reference state is dominated by the Hartree–Fock determinant, operators that act directly on this component generate basis directions with relatively large amplitudes, resulting in larger overlap eigenvalues.
In contrast, operators that annihilate the dominant Hartree-Fock component, or act primarily on the smaller correlated components of the reference state, generate correspondingly smaller eigenvalues.

This also explains the contrasting behavior of the occupied number operators in the singlet and triplet sectors. This is particularly consequential for 1-UpCCGSD, which only allows for singles and paired doubles excitations. Its spectrum, shown in \cref{fig:si-1up-triplet-eigenvalues}, highlights the corresponding $M$ number-operator bases appearing as near-null eigenvalues.

A second source of near-null eigenvalues occurs in the $2e2o$, $2e3o$, and $4e3o$ active spaces. 
In those cases, the number of nominal QSE basis states exceeds the dimension of the physically accessible subspace within the relevant spin sector. 
This results in redundant linear combinations, which manifest as near-null eigenvalues of the overlap matrix $\mathbf{S}$. 
More generally, this occurs for small active spaces in which the orbital window does not extend beyond $\mathrm{HOMO}\pm1$ and $\mathrm{LUMO}\pm1$. 
The resulting near-null eigenvalues are therefore not caused by weak operator support alone, but can arise simply from the dimensionality constraint of the many-electron Hilbert space.

\subsubsection{Significance of Small Eigenvalues}
Taken together, these trends reveal a recurring block of comparatively large overlap eigenvalues associated with operator classes whose action on the reference state produces basis states with relatively large weights identified above.
The corresponding stable dimensions are therefore approximately $1+n_{\mathrm{occ}}n_{\mathrm{vir}}$ for singlets and $3n_{\mathrm{occ}}n_{\mathrm{vir}}$ for triplets, with the remaining basis states progressively less significant. 
We term these basis states \textit{stable} because their associated overlap eigenvalues remain consistently of magnitude one or larger. Retaining them therefore does not introduce any strong noise amplification.

This naturally raises the question of whether these \textit{stable} basis states alone are sufficient to recover most of the low-energy QSE spectrum. 
This is of significant interest because a consistently stable rank would provide a natural basis for thresholding, particularly when shot-sampling noise is introduced.
To test this, we restrict the QSE subspace to be spanned by the stable basis states identified above and solve the resulting generalized eigenvalue problem.
However, this restriction severely degrades the recovered spectrum, with the median errors across the low-lying states increasing by $6.1$--$13.8 \mathrm{mHa}$ (\cref{fig:si-max-stable-error-distribution}). 

This suggests that while the stable basis states form a well-conditioned core of the QSE subspace, they themselves are insufficient to recover the low-lying states accurately.
The remaining basis states with seemingly smaller amplitudes contribute important correlation effects that cannot be trivially discarded in order to achieve chemical accuracy.

\subsection{Finite-Shot Effects on Generalized Eigenvalue Stability}
This trade-off becomes particularly important under finite-shot sampling, where the near-null region of the overlap spectrum is most strongly perturbed, resulting in instances where the smallest eigenvalues turn negative (\cref{fig:si-eigenvalue-lifting}). 
This motivates the use of overlap-eigenvalue thresholding to remove weakly supported eigenmodes.
In this section, we examine how thresholding can be used appropriately to regularize QSE under finite-shot noise.

\Cref{fig:threshold-shot-error} shows a clear minimum in the QSE error as a function of the overlap-eigenvalue threshold, indicating that an optimal degree of regularization exists for each noise level. 
As the number of shots increases and the sampling noise decreases, this minimum shifts towards lower thresholds and lower overall errors.

This behavior reflects the competing effects of thresholding. 
Eigenbases associated with very small overlap eigenvalues are particularly susceptible to noise amplification, such that their contribution to the recovered spectrum can become dominated by statistical error rather than useful physical information. 
Increasing the threshold initially removes these poorly resolved eigenbases and therefore reduces the QSE error. 
However, beyond the optimum, further increasing the threshold begins to remove more prominent eigenbases that contain physically important contributions to the QSE subspace, causing the error to increase again. 
The resulting minimum therefore represents a balance between suppressing noise-amplified directions and retaining sufficient information for accurate spectral recovery (\cref{fig:si-optimal-threshold}).

Increasing the shot count improves the precision of the projected matrix elements and therefore allows progressively smaller overlap eigenvalues to be resolved reliably. Consequently, fewer directions need to be removed, shifting the optimal threshold towards smaller values as the shot count increases.

Since the spectral norm is bounded above by the Frobenius norm, the growth of the accumulated matrix perturbation sets a natural upper scale for the resulting QSE energy errors.

\subsection{Scaling and Future Work}
Taken together, these results point to several challenges that must be addressed before QSE can be extended reliably to larger active spaces. 
Most fundamentally, the operator-generated subspace must remain sufficiently expressive to represent the target low-energy states, yet the statevector results show a clear degradation in representability as the active space is enlarged.
At the same time, the finite-shot calculations reveal a steep increase in measurement requirements with the problem size.
In the following section, we consider several approaches for addressing these limitations and improving the scalability of QSE. 

\subsubsection{Addition of OV Doubles Operators}
A major source of the representability errors identified above is insufficient independent support for double-excitation character within the one-body QSE subspace. 
States with substantial double-excitation contributions can therefore remain poorly represented even in the absence of finite-shot noise. 
A clear approach is to increase the expressiveness of the generated subspace by introducing explicit double-excitation operators. 
Here, we examine this possibility at the $6e6o$ active space using an expanded operator pool comprising the original one-body excitations (\crefrange{eq:qse-singlet-operator-definition}{eq:qse-singlet-operator-pool}) together with a restricted set of occupied-to-virtual (OV) double excitations (\crefrange{eq:qse-singlet-ov-doubles-operator}{eq:qse-singlet-ov-doubles-operator-pool}).
We focus this analysis on $S_1$, as it exhibits the largest representability errors among the low-lying states considered. 
We restricted the operator pool to the OV sector because these excitations are expected to capture the dominant double-excitation pathways while keeping the calculation computationally tractable.

Nevertheless, approximately half the molecules remain above chemical accuracy, with strongly conjugated systems like Octatetraene and Hexatriene retaining errors above $10 \mathrm{mHa}$. 
This indicates that the restricted OV doubles excitations alone are not sufficient to fully repair the representability gap.
Additional two-body excitations may therefore be required to provide the missing flexibility, which could be important for strongly multiconfigurational states whose structure is not fully captured by the dominant OV sector alone. 
A similar effect is observed for the one-body operator pool, where OV excitations provide the dominant contributions, but non-OV operators still make appreciable contributions toward the generated subspace.  

However, a complete doubles operator pool would substantially enlarge the projected problem and is beyond the scope of the present calculations. 
Assessing the extent to which the remaining representability errors can be recovered by the full two-body operator manifold is therefore left for future work, particularly in larger and more strongly multiconfigurational active spaces. 
Despite these remaining errors, the present results demonstrate that explicit double-excitation operators can markedly improve QSE representability, supporting higher-rank operator expansions as a promising direction.

\subsubsection{Structure of the Projected Matrices}
That being said, a doubles operator pool (even the restricted OV form) scales as $\mathcal{O}\!\left(n_{\mathrm{orb}}^{4}\right)$, compared with $\mathcal{O}\!\left(n_{\mathrm{orb}}^{2}\right)$ for the present one-body pool. 
This has important consequences for measurement scaling. 
As the dimension of the projected problem now grows significantly faster with $n_{\mathrm{orb}}$, the measurement requirements are expected to increase even more steeply than for the one-body construction underlying the $\mathcal{O}\!\left(n_{\mathrm{orb}}^{11}\right)$ estimate in \cref{sec:shot-budget-scaling}. 
This further highlights the central tension: increasing the expressiveness of the operator pool can improve representability, but at the cost of a rapidly increasing measurement burden. 
Promisingly, the projected matrices themselves exhibit substantial structure that may provide opportunities for more efficient reconstruction and measurement. 
In the remainder of this section, we further examine the recurring patterns and correlations within the projected $\mathbf{H}$ and $\mathbf{S}$ matrices.

We first observe a strong correlation between corresponding elements of the projected $\mathbf{H}$ and $\mathbf{S}$ matrices, with $R^2=0.99$ (\cref{fig:si-h-s-element-correlation}). 
In general, elements with small overlap ${S}_{ij}$ also tend to have small Hamiltonian couplings ${H}_{ij}$. 
This suggests that the Hamiltonian predominantly couples pairs of operator-generated states that already have appreciable overlap within the QSE subspace. 

The matrices also exhibit pronounced internal structure. The overlap-matrix heatmaps in \cref{fig:si-full-singlet-heatmaps,fig:si-full-triplet-heatmaps} show consistently large diagonal elements, as expected from the self-overlap of the operator-generated states. 
The off-diagonal elements are much less uniform: stronger couplings appear in recurring block-like regions associated with particular classes of excitation operators, leaving other regions weakly coupled or nearly empty.
The degree of sparsity also varies substantially between matrices. 
However, we find no clear relationship between this sparsity and the molecular properties considered across the benchmark, which suggests that the patterns could depend on other factors like the chosen active space and included orbitals.

When we perturb the matrices with finite-shot sampling noise (\cref{fig:shot-overlap-structure}), we observe that the noise progressively obscures the detailed patterns present in the statevector overlap matrices. 
The dominant diagonal elements remain visible even at relatively low shot counts, whereas the finer off-diagonal and block-like structures are substantially blurred. 
Increasing the number of shots gradually restores these features, with the reconstructed matrices approaching the underlying statevector structure more closely at higher sampling precision.

Beyond the element-wise structure of the projected matrices, we also observe that the sampling process itself introduces a further form of structure through correlated errors between $\mathbf{H}$ and $\mathbf{S}$.
Throughout this study, commuting Pauli terms were grouped globally across both matrices, such that shared observables were estimated from the same measurement outcomes. 
This induces correlated sampling errors between $\mathbf{H}$ and $\mathbf{S}$.
To assess their importance, we deliberately break these correlations by solving the generalized eigenvalue problem using noisy $\mathbf{H}$ and $\mathbf{S}$ matrices obtained from independent measurements. 
We find that the resulting errors increase substantially by about four orders of magnitude (\cref{fig:si-breaking-h-s-correlated-errors}). 
More strikingly, combining a finite-shot $\mathbf{H}$ matrix with an exact statevector $\mathbf{S}$ matrix can produce larger errors than using finite-shot estimates of both matrices. 
This counterintuitive result indicates that correlated perturbations in $\mathbf{H}$ and $\mathbf{S}$ can partially compensate within the generalized eigenvalue problem, and that preserving these correlations is important for accurate QSE reconstruction.

This distinction is particularly important when comparing with previous studies that reported improved accuracy when replacing the noisy overlap matrix with its exact statevector counterpart \cite{Asthana2023QscEOM}. 
In those studies, sampling uncertainty was modelled by adding random perturbations directly and independently to individual matrix elements, under the assumption that the elements experience errors of comparable magnitude. 
However, our results suggest that such simplified noise models alone may be insufficient to accurately represent finite-shot QSE, and that future simulations should preserve the correlations induced by the underlying measurement and grouping protocol.

\subsubsection{Outlook for Structure-Aware reconstruction}
The observations above suggest that finite-shot QSE should not simply be treated as a problem of reconstructing two dense matrices from independent noisy estimates. 
The corresponding elements of $\mathbf{H}$ and $\mathbf{S}$ are strongly correlated, the statevector matrices exhibit recurring internal structure, and the finite-shot perturbations themselves are correlated through the shared measurement groups. 
As such, the projected QSE problem is highly structured at both the signal and noise levels.

We envisage that this structure may provide opportunities for classical post-processing methods to reconstruct the projected matrices more accurately from a limited number of measurements. 
Related approaches have already been explored in quantum error mitigation, where machine-learning, model-based and constraint-based methods exploit systematic structure, correlations or known physical constraints in noisy measurement data to improve estimates of ideal observables \cite{Liao2024MLQEM, Aasen2025ReadoutMitigation, Wang2026RDMErrorPostProcessing}. 
These methods are effective because the targeted errors are not treated as completely independent fluctuations, but instead contain patterns that can be learned or explicitly modelled. 

Therefore, although QSE exhibits steep measurement scaling under present direct-estimation strategies, the substantial structure observed in the projected matrices provides a positive outlook for improving measurement efficiency. 
Structure-aware reconstruction methods that exploit these recurring features may achieve comparable spectral accuracy with substantially fewer measurements.
