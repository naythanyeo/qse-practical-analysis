% PARA 1: Why Quantum Computing 
Computational chemistry has become inseparable from modern chemical research, accelerating the pace at which new compounds, materials, and chemical hypotheses can be evaluated. Electronic-structure calculations underpin much of this progress through molecular-property prediction, high-throughput screening, and atomistic simulation \cite{blum2024ESRoadmap, Marzari2021ESApplications,Burke2012DFTReview}. 
However, systematically accurate wavefunction-based methods become prohibitively expensive for large systems because the dimension of the exact configuration space grows combinatorially. Practical classical methods like Density Functional Theory (DFT) rely on approximations whose accuracy is not uniformly reliable across different correlation regimes \cite{Burke2012DFTReview}. 
Quantum computers instead offer an alternative route to modelling nature - which is inherently quantum mechanical - by representing and manipulating quantum states using quantum hardware. Qubits encode and manipulate superpositions of many basis states that would generally require exponentially many amplitudes to represent on a classical machine \cite{Schleich2025QuantumComputingForQuantumChemistry,McArdle2020QuantumComputingChemistryReview}.

% PARA 2: Quantum Computing Challenges and Methods
The current generation of quantum hardware, albeit rapidly advancing, remains constrained by imperfect gate operations and limited coherent circuit depth. 
These limitations have motivated hybrid quantum-classical algorithms that use quantum processors for state preparation and measurement, while delegating parameter optimisation and post-processing to classical hardware \cite{Sharma2020VQENoiseResilience}. Among the most prominent is the Variational Quantum Eigensolver (VQE), which minimises the expectation value of the Hamiltonian to approximate the lowest-energy state accessible to a chosen ansatz \cite{Kumar2025VQEReview}.
Quantum Subspace Methods (QSM) build on this idea by projecting the full eigenvalue problem onto a compact trial subspace. If the chosen subspace accurately captures the low-energy manifold, the target ground and excited state solutions can be recovered without diagonalising the full many-body Hamiltonian \cite{Motta2024QSMReview}.

% PARA 5: Limitations of QSE
In general, the operator-generated basis states are not orthogonal, such that $\mathbf{S} \neq \mathbf{I}$. As such, solving this generalised eigenvalue problem requires a whitening transformation containing factors proportional to $\frac{1}{\sqrt{\lambda_i}}$, whereby $\lambda_i$ are the eigenvalues of $\mathbf{S}$. 
If the generated basis states are nearly linearly-dependent,  $\mathbf{S}$ develops very small or even near-null eigenvalues. 
Consequently, measurement errors in the matrix elements are strongly amplified, rendering the projected problem numerically unstable. This ill-conditioning of $\mathbf{S}$ is a well-known limitation of QSE, and particularly consequential because statistical shot noise is inherent to real quantum hardware measurements. 

% PARA 6: Then given that, why still bother with QSE
Alternative QSM formulations like Quantum Self-Consistent Equation-Of-Motion (Q-SC-EOM) methods avoid this source of instability by constructing an orthogonal excitation manifold \cite{Asthana2023QscEOM}. This reduces the overlap matrix to the identity, and consequently the generalised eigenvalue problem to an ordinary one.
Doing so has been shown to outperform QSE in spectral accuracy especially when the condition number of $\mathbf{S}$ is large \cite{Kwao2026QSMGeneralisedEigenvalueProblem}. 
QSE nevertheless remains of practical interest due to its direct and comparatively flexible formulation. 
While q-sc-EOM is formulated primarily to recover excitation energy differences relative to a reference state, QSE yields Ritz approximations to absolute energies. QSE therefore serves a dual purpose within the same projected calculation: refinement of the approximate ground state through the lowest Ritz solution, and recovery of excited-state approximations through the higher solutions \cite{mcclean2017qse,yoshioka2022GeneralisedQSE}.
Furthermore, QSE does not require its expansion operators to satisfy the vacuum-annihilation condition imposed in self-consistent EOM formulations, allowing a wider range of physically motivated operator pools to be considered. This flexibility leaves substantial scope for tailoring the generated subspace and improving the balance between spectral representability and numerical stability.

Much of the existing QSE literature has evaluated the method using selected molecular systems, often at distorted geometries chosen to probe strongly correlated or numerically challenging regimes. Although these studies have established important limitations in subspace representability and numerical stability, it remains unclear how frequently such limitations arise across chemically diverse molecules under more routine equilibrium conditions. Recent work has also shown that overlap-matrix thresholding can suppress instabilities in the generalized eigenvalue problem. However, the appropriate regularization generally depends on both the underlying system and the level of measurement noise, while overly aggressive thresholding may remove physically relevant subspace directions. A broader characterization is therefore needed to determine how QSE accuracy, representability, and numerical recoverability behave across molecular systems, and how regularization should be applied in practice.

In this work, we address these questions through a systematic benchmark of spin-resolved QSE across 28 chemically varied molecules \cite{thiels2008benchmark}. Statevector simulations are first used to isolate the intrinsic representability and accuracy of the operator-generated subspace. We compare several reference ansatz classes, ranging from restricted to more expressive constructions, to determine how the prepared reference influences the recoverable spectrum. We then analyse the origins and practical consequences of overlap-matrix ill-conditioning under finite-shot sampling, including its dependence on the overlap spectrum and regularization threshold. Finally, we quantify the associated scaling and measurement requirements, provide practical guidance for threshold selection, and identify directions for improving the construction and numerical treatment of QSE subspaces. The principal contribution of this work is therefore a systematic assessment of when molecular QSE remains both sufficiently expressive and numerically recoverable for practical excited-state calculations.
